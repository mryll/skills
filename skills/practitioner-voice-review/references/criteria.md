## What Practitioner Voice is

The article defines it as "a discipline: start with the claim, stay visible, ground authority in consequence, compress without losing meaning, and leave real tension live." The goal "is not to sound more personal. The goal is to make earned judgment useful to readers who have lived the problem."

"Practitioner" means anyone whose authority comes from "having done the work and lived with what happened next". The field does not matter; the relation to consequence does.

## Three ways to disappear

Highsmith names three ways the author's judgment leaves the prose. Use them to describe *where* judgment went, not to label the author.

- **Rigor as camouflage (academic).** Scaffolding first: define, contextualize, qualify, then deploy. Literature and caveats stand in for a position. "The problem isn't academic rigor. The problem is when rigor becomes camouflage."
- **Polish as camouflage (thought leadership).** Hook, three lessons, call to action. Brand over argument, engagement over truth. Tension gets resolved in the takeaways.
- **LLM Voice.** AI produces writing that is "fluent, well-organized, correctly structured, optimized to say nothing wrong." The most insidious, because it is "indistinguishable from the real thing until you notice nothing is at stake." Signal to look for: no decision anyone could be held to, no cost anyone accepted, no context that limits the claim. Describe the absence of stakes; never claim the text was machine-written.

## Core principles

### 1. Start with the claim

Practitioner Voice opens with the conclusion and "lets the rest of the piece be explanation, not proof." The reader who has lived the pattern recognizes it; scaffolding will not convince the one who has not.

Ask: can you state the claim in three sentences? Where does it first appear? Does the body explain the claim or try to earn it?

Genre note (skill's adaptation): in an ADR the claim is the decision; an early Decision section satisfies this.

### 2. Stay visible

"In Practitioner Voice the author's judgment is the point. Not the data. Not the framework." Frameworks, data and literature "earn their place when they serve your judgment. They become camouflage when they replace it."

Ask: where does the author choose, rank or reject something, and say why? Could this text have been assembled from documentation and best-practice lists? An impersonal text can still pass: judgment shows in decisions, rejections and accepted costs, not in pronouns.

### 3. Authority from consequence

Authority comes "from having been responsible for an outcome, not studying it, not advising on it from a safe distance". "Show the scar": failure, learned from, is a credential; academic writing quarantines it in a limitations section and thought leadership hides it.

For consultants, the article says authority is "Earned differently. Still earned.": proximity to consequence across many contexts. This skill extends the same reading to architects and reviewers who do not own outcomes.

Ask: is a claim grounded in something observed, done or lived with, or only in citations and authority? Is a real consequence present where the argument needs one? Never infer that the author lacks experience because the text does not narrate it, and never ask for a scar that may not exist; ask whether one does.

### 4. Leave real tension live

"Name it. Illuminate it. Step back. Do not resolve it for the reader; resolving it would be lying." The article's own example: two principled, opposite positions on AI in writing, both kept standing.

Ask: are competing defensible positions named, or is one option sold as costless? Is a tension closed artificially with a takeaway? Genre note (skill's adaptation): an ADR resolves the choice; the trade-offs the choice accepts stay visible.

### 5. Compression as respect

"Write as if the reader already knows more than they can easily articulate. Fewer definitions. Shorter transitions." "The scaffolding isn't missing. The reader is the scaffolding." But "A piece is as long as the tension requires and no longer. Short is not a virtue; unnecessary length is a failure."

Ask: what could an experienced reader skip without loss? What does the target reader need that is missing? Never recommend removing information someone needs to implement or audit a decision.

### 6. The readership contract: specificity over universality

Practitioner Voice writes "for people who recognize the pattern from their own experience", and the article widens it: "You are writing for the reader who has lived this pattern or will." Do not strip context that the reader who has not lived it yet still needs. It is "comfortable with limited context — this worked here, under these conditions, judge for yourself whether it fits yours."

Ask: does the text bound where its claims hold? Does a local conclusion turn into "everyone should"? Is the reader precise, or "everyone"?

### 7. Information and inspiration blended

"The information arrives inside the inspiration." When a section is purely inspirational or purely informational, "that's the signal. Blend it back."

Ask: does the text switch gears between a story block and a data block, or does the judgment carry the evidence?

## Craft resources (genre-dependent)

Use these only where the genre invites them. Their absence is not a defect in a spec or an ADR.

- **Story as delivery mechanism.** "A story doesn't decorate an argument. It is the argument's delivery mechanism, the thing that makes the reader feel tension before they understand it." Know what happened, who was there, the stakes, what changed. Stories can come from outside work (terrain, physical consequence).
- **Braided narrative.** Several threads that develop independently and meet. Worth suggesting only when the author is forcing parallel tracks into one sequence.
- **Back cover / lede first.** Who is it for, what will they get, why now. If the claim does not fit in three sentences, "the piece isn't ready to draft."
- **Carry phrase.** A closing line that reorients rather than summarizes. Optional; an inverted pyramid does not need one.
- **Person.** In the article's comparison, Practitioner Voice "starts in first person and shifts to second only when the reader needs to feel themselves in the situation." A tendency, not a rule; never require first person.
- **Citations.** "identify gaps early, fix mechanics late." Name the source in prose when relevant, full reference at the end. "Citations exist because ideas belong to people." Missing or doubtful attribution of someone else's idea is worth one line, as a question for the author to check; this review does not research sources, so it never states that an attribution is right or wrong. Citation formatting is not this review's job.

## Line-level signals

Highsmith orders editing as developmental (does the piece work?), then line (does each paragraph carry one clear idea?), then proofreading (is it clean?). This skill covers the first two and leaves the third out: a list of typos and formatting slips is a different job, and it would bury the argument-level findings that the author needs first. Report line signals only after the argument-level findings, with a quote, without replacement wording.

- **Self-signposting.** The text announces its own moves: "So let us name it" (the article's example), or phrases like "In this section we will" and "Let's dive in" (the skill's examples). Practitioner Voice "trusts the reader to track the argument without being told where it's going."
- **Heritage tour.** A separate section of intellectual lineage before the point. In the article's comparison, the lineage belongs "in one paragraph, inside the argument, not as a separate tour."
- **Jargon as tribal signal.** A term the reader does not share, used without translation, "signals tribal membership instead of transferring meaning." Context calibration: with a mixed audience, define a term once in plain words, then use it. A technical term earns its place by "doing something a plain word cannot."
- **Static sentences.** Many sentences built on "is" and "was" instead of what things do; gerunds where a direct verb works; "that" used heavily. These are English signals. For a text in another language, look for the equivalent habit (in Spanish: chains of "ser/estar", gerunds, nominalizations, stacked "que" clauses) and only when you know that language's style norms.
- **Pace.** All sentences the same length; punctuation chosen without regard to what it does to pace ("A period is a stop sign. A colon announces something ahead. A dash is a tree branch in the road.").
- **Thought-leadership tells.** Hooks, numbered lessons, motivational connectors, "follow for more".

## Reading the evidence

None of these signals proves a problem on its own. Before you recommend a change:

1. Does the criticism rest on a quote or a real section?
2. Is it missing evidence on the page, or missing experience? You can only see the first.
3. Does the genre and the reader actually need what you are asking for?
4. Does the recommendation leave the decision with the author, as a question?
5. Is any sentence in your review replacement prose, a score, or a guess about authorship? Remove it.
